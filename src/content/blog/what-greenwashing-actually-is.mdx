The word "greenwashing" carries a built-in accusation: someone knew a claim was false and printed it anyway.

That's a comforting way to understand it, for two different audiences. Readers get to feel like sophisticated skeptics catching a villain. Companies that aren't lying on purpose get to assume the word doesn't apply to them.

Neither assumption holds up against how greenwashing is actually being enforced right now. Across a dense run of settlements and rulings from August 2025 through mid-2026 — Arizona, Philadelphia, California, the European Commission, Italy's competition authority, Australia's securities regulator, French courts — almost none of the cases turned on proving a company set out to deceive anyone.

They turned on a much lower bar: whether a reasonable consumer, reading the label as printed, would form an impression that didn't match reality. Intent was rarely the question. Verification was.

---

## The Pattern Regulators Keep Finding

Start with the recycling symbol, because it's where the enforcement volume has been heaviest.

Arizona's attorney general settled with Reynolds Consumer Products for $212,000 in February 2026 over Hefty-branded "recycling" bags — packaging with bins-full-of-bottles imagery and language suggesting the bags belonged in curbside recycling. The bags were polyethylene film, a material almost no municipal recycling facility accepts, because it jams sorting equipment.

The state didn't have to show Reynolds wanted to sabotage recycling streams. It had to show the packaging created a false impression of what would happen to the bag after use — and that consumers acted on that impression, contaminating actual recyclables in the process.

Philadelphia's September 2025 suit against SC Johnson and Bimbo Bakeries makes the same argument about "recyclable" labels on Ziploc-style bags and bread wraps: flexible film that the city's own materials recovery facilities can't process, wrapped around machinery instead of getting sorted.

California's attorney general reached a similar conclusion in October 2025, settling with four plastic-bag manufacturers for a combined $1.75 million over chasing-arrows symbols on bags with no real end-of-life pathway in the state. Under its truth-in-recyclability statute, a claim can be actionable simply because it's misleading in the market where it's sold — whatever the person who approved the label copy believed at the time.

The European Commission's action against Coca-Cola in May 2025 runs on the same logic from a different angle. "100% recycled plastic bottle" was true of the bottle body. It wasn't true of the cap, the label, or the adhesive — components an average shopper has no reason to mentally subtract before reading the claim as covering the whole product.

The fix the EU required wasn't a fine for lying. It was a requirement to state the exclusion plainly. That's a tell about what the underlying problem actually was: not fabrication, but a claim that was accurate at the component level and never checked against what a reasonable person would understand it to mean at the product level.

## What Nobody Checked, and What Nobody Could Have Missed

Not every case in this window looks the same once you ask what actually went wrong.

In the recycling-label cases, the likeliest failure is that nobody routed the claim through the system it described before it shipped. A packaging or brand team wrote "recyclable" because the resin is, in principle, capable of being recycled somewhere — and never checked it against the accepted-materials list of the municipalities where the product would actually be sold and discarded.

That's a verification gap: a claim built on what's technically true rather than what's operationally true where the product lands. It doesn't require bad faith. It requires nobody owning the question of whether the claim survives contact with the actual disposal system — a data and process failure, not a moral one.

Other cases in the same window don't fit that pattern. Denmark's Western High Court found that Danish Crown's "climate-controlled pork" campaign had no independently verified methodology behind it at all — not a partial truth, not a system mismatch, just an unsupported comparative claim. The company subsequently conceded the point rather than continuing to defend it.

Australia's securities regulator sued Active Super over holdings in coal, Russian oil and gas, and tobacco sitting inside a fund marketed on excluding exactly those categories; a federal court agreed in June 2024. A French court ordered TotalEnergies to remove net-zero messaging after finding it overstated progress the company's own fossil-fuel expansion contradicted.

In each of those, the gap wasn't between a claim and a system nobody checked. It was between a claim and a record the company already had. Someone could have looked at the fund's actual holdings, or the actual emissions trajectory, and didn't — or did, and the marketing copy shipped anyway.

That distinction is the one worth keeping straight, because it's the one "accidental greenwashing" flattens. A claim nobody verified against reality, and a claim that contradicts records the company already held, are both getting called greenwashing. Only one of those is close to a lie. The other is closer to a company that never built a source of truth for the claims it was making — and found that out the same way its customers did, after the label was printed.

## Why This Is a Data Problem Before It's a Messaging Problem

The instinct, once a company sees this pattern, is to route sustainability claims through legal review before they ship. That helps, but it treats the symptom.

Legal review can catch a claim that's obviously unsupportable. It can't manufacture the underlying evidence a defensible claim actually needs. A lawyer reading "recyclable" can't tell you whether the municipalities where the product sells actually accept that material, any more than a copywriter can. That's not a knowledge gap either of them is positioned to close — it's a data gap, and it sits upstream of both legal review and marketing.

The recurring shape across this entire enforcement window is a company that had, or could have gotten, the specific fact its claim depended on: what the accepted-materials list actually says in its sale markets, what a fund's actual holdings are, what a supply chain's actual emissions trajectory looks like, what an offset project's actual permanence assumptions are. Either nobody checked it, or someone checked it somewhere the claim's authors never saw.

Reasonable-consumer standards don't ask whether that gap was intentional. They ask whether the claim matched reality — and reality doesn't grade on effort.

---

## What This Means Before You Publish a Claim

None of this argues for saying less. Companies with real sustainability and emissions data are also pulling back right now, largely out of the same anxiety — worried that any claim invites the scrutiny that sank Hefty's packaging or Coca-Cola's label. That's the wrong lesson from this enforcement window.

The claims that held up weren't the vague ones. They were the ones with a specific, checkable fact behind them, stated at the level the fact actually supports — a bottle body, not the whole bottle; a fund's screened universe, stated accurately against its actual holdings; a reduction target, backed by a trajectory a court wouldn't have to order you to disclose.

That's a verification exercise, not a copywriting one: knowing, before a claim ships, exactly what record it rests on — and whether that record would survive being read the way a regulator, or a customer's own sustainability team, is now reading it.

It matters on the buyer side too, not just the regulatory one. A claim a customer has learned to discount doesn't just risk a fine — it stops functioning as a reason to buy at all, which is [one of the documented mechanics behind why stated sustainability values so often don't convert into a purchase](/blog/why-esg-communications-fall-flat/).
