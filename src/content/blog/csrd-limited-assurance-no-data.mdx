**"No data" rarely survives limited assurance for Scope 3 Category 1.** Category 1 covers purchased goods and services. The spend-based method needs only what you paid each supplier, and that's already in your own accounts payable ledger.

Limited assurance is the level of review CSRD requires. The practitioner gathers enough evidence to say nothing came to their attention suggesting the report is materially misstated. It's lighter than a financial audit, but it still tests gaps.

## What the Assurance Provider Does When They Hit "No Data"

1. **Check whether the gap is justified.** They look at your double materiality assessment. Was the category assessed as not material, or is it material and simply missing? They also check that the process you describe matches what you actually did.
2. **Ask for the documentation.** That means the materiality assessment, the reasoning for any exclusion, and a record of what you did to try to get the data. If you're relying on "undue cost or effort," they'll want that assessment too.
3. **Check whether the data was actually available.** For Category 1, the question is usually "was the spend in your ledger?" If it was, "no data" becomes "data we didn't use," which is a much weaker position.
4. **Decide what it does to the conclusion.** A material, unjustified gap means a modified conclusion. It's a **qualified conclusion** if the issue isn't pervasive, and an **adverse conclusion** or **disclaimer** if it is. A disclosed, justified gap may need only an emphasis of matter paragraph, or no change at all.

> **What this page covers:** how a CSRD limited assurance provider treats missing Scope 3 data, with Category 1 as the main example.
> **Who it's for:** sustainability, finance and reporting teams preparing CSRD sustainability statements.
> **Key sources:** CEAOB guidelines on limited assurance (September 30, 2024), Accountancy Europe on value chain information (December 2024), Cooley on the revised ESRS (July 2026), BDO on CSRD after the Omnibus (April 2026).
> **Note on terms:** limited assurance ends in a *conclusion*, not an *opinion*. A "qualified finding" isn't a formal outcome. The formal outcomes are unmodified, qualified, adverse and disclaimer.

## The Possible Outcomes

| Situation | Likely result |
|---|---|
| Gap is not material, or is justified and clearly disclosed | Unmodified conclusion. Possibly an emphasis of matter paragraph. |
| Company used allowed transitional relief for value chain data | No scope limitation required |
| Material misstatement or omission, not pervasive | Qualified conclusion |
| Material misstatement or omission, pervasive | Adverse conclusion |
| Practitioner can't get enough evidence, material but not pervasive | Qualified conclusion |
| Practitioner can't get enough evidence, material and pervasive | Disclaimer of conclusion |

## Step 1: Is the Gap Justified?

The CEAOB guidelines tell practitioners to understand the process you used to decide what goes in your sustainability statement. They check that the process you describe matches reality and meets the ESRS. If they believe a material impact, risk or opportunity is missing, the limited assurance report should say so. [Slaughter and May](https://sustainability.slaughterandmay.com/post/102jnna/sustainability-reporting-ceaob-publishes-guidelines-on-limited-assurance)

So the first question isn't "do you have data?" It's "did your materiality assessment decide this category matters?" If it did, a blank is a problem. If it didn't, the practitioner checks that the decision was reasonable.

## Step 2: Show Your Work

Under the original ESRS, which apply to reports for financial years up to 2026, companies had to make reasonable efforts to get value chain information. If they still couldn't, they had to disclose that. [Accountancy Europe](https://accountancyeurope.eu/publications/csrd-readiness-limited-assurance-approach-to-value-chain-information/)

The revised ESRS, applying from financial year 2027, drop the "reasonable efforts" test. They replace it with a rule to use "all reasonable and supportable information" available at the reporting date, without undue cost or effort. That assessment must be redone each period. For the first three years, where value chain information is incomplete, companies must explain the efforts they made. [Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-07-21-european-commission-adopts-revised-eu-csrd-reporting-standards)

Either way, the practitioner will ask for a paper trail.

## Step 3: Was the Data Really Unavailable?

This is where Category 1 claims usually fall apart.

Most value chain data, like a supplier's own emissions, sits outside your company. Spend data doesn't. What you paid for cloud, software, AI tools and services is in your AP, expense and card systems. The spend-based method turns that into an estimate. It multiplies spend by an industry emission factor, such as the US EPA's 0.093 kg CO2e per dollar for data processing and hosting. See [the spend-based method](/blog/carbon-accounting-ai-services-spend-based-method/).

The revised ESRS also say value chain data and assumptions should, where possible, be consistent with those used for the financial statements. [Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-07-21-european-commission-adopts-revised-eu-csrd-reporting-standards) Your spend data is already in the financial statements. It's hard to argue it was too costly to use.

For how to get that spend from finance to the sustainability team, see [The Finance-to-ESG Data Pipeline](/blog/finance-to-esg-data-pipeline/).

## Step 4: What It Does to the Conclusion

If a material gap isn't justified, the conclusion is modified. The CEAOB guidelines describe a qualified conclusion when misstatements aren't pervasive and an adverse conclusion when they are. [Slaughter and May](https://sustainability.slaughterandmay.com/post/102jnna/sustainability-reporting-ceaob-publishes-guidelines-on-limited-assurance)

If the practitioner simply can't get enough evidence, that's a scope limitation. It leads to a qualified conclusion if material but not pervasive, or a disclaimer if material and pervasive. In other cases, an emphasis of matter paragraph may be enough. [Accountancy Europe](https://accountancyeurope.eu/publications/csrd-readiness-limited-assurance-approach-to-value-chain-information/)

## What the Omnibus Changed About Assurance

- **Limited assurance stays.** It's still required from the first year of reporting. [BDO](https://www.bdo.com/insights/sustainability-and-esg/csrd-post-omnibus-revised-scope-and-requirements)
- **Reasonable assurance is off the table.** The planned later move to reasonable assurance was removed. [BDO](https://www.bdo.com/insights/sustainability-and-esg/csrd-post-omnibus-revised-scope-and-requirements)
- **EU assurance standards are coming.** EU limited assurance standards must be adopted by July 2027. Until then, practitioners rely on national standards, the CEAOB guidelines and international standards such as ISSA 5000. [BDO](https://www.bdo.com/insights/sustainability-and-esg/csrd-post-omnibus-revised-scope-and-requirements)
- **Fewer companies are in scope.** CSRD now covers companies above 1,000 employees and €450 million in turnover. See the [ESG Regulatory Status Tracker](/blog/esg-regulatory-status-tracker/).

## How to Avoid a "No Data" Problem

1. **Run the spend-based method for Category 1 first.** It's the floor. Refine with supplier-specific data later.
2. **Write down the materiality reasoning** for every Scope 3 category you exclude.
3. **Keep an effort log.** Who you asked, when and what came back.
4. **Use estimates, and say they're estimates.** Name the factor source and version.
5. **Reassess each year.** "Undue cost or effort" doesn't carry over automatically.
