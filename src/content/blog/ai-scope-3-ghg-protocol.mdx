**Stage 2: Exploration**

Your finance team has a rapidly growing AI budget. Your ESG team has a Scope 3 target and a looming CSRD/SBTi disclosure deadline. The question in the middle is simple and under-answered: *where does AI usage actually sit in your Scope 3 inventory, and what data do you need to treat it credibly?*

This piece answers that question in GHG Protocol terms, with enough technical depth that your sustainability team and your controller can both act on it—and with a clear bridge into a follow-up, "How to Calculate AI Emissions" guide.

---

## 1. How the GHG Protocol Corporate Value Chain Standard Sees AI

The **GHG Protocol Corporate Value Chain (Scope 3) Standard** is still your reference point in 2026. It splits Scope 3 into 15 categories, eight upstream and seven downstream. Two features matter most for AI usage:

- **Category 1 – Purchased Goods and Services**  
  Defined as emissions from *all purchased goods and services* not included in other upstream categories. That includes everything from raw materials to *software and cloud services* your business buys [1][2].

- **Scope 3's relative size**  
  Multiple analyses find Scope 3 averages **75–90% of a company's total footprint**, with Category 1 typically the **largest single category** or co-largest with Category 11 (use of sold products) for many sectors [3][4].

Within that framework, AI usage is conceptually straightforward: if you **buy AI as a service** rather than operating the hardware yourself, the emissions from running those models are **upstream, third-party emissions**. That is exactly what Category 1 is for.

---

## 2. Why AI API Spend Sits in Scope 3 Category 1

Across recent guidance, the classification is converging:

- **Purchased AI services are Scope 3, Category 1**  
  Multiple 2025–2026 sources—SINAI, Hobi, Asuene, Watershed, Verdantix—state that AI used as a purchased service belongs in **Scope 3, Category 1 (Purchased Goods and Services)** [3][5][6][7].  

  Common formulations include:
  - "For most companies that use AI as a purchased service, AI emissions fall under Scope 3, Category 1, purchased goods and services" [5].  
  - "Paid AI subscriptions (ChatGPT Plus, Claude Pro, Google One AI) sit in Scope 3, Category 1… API usage sits in the same category" [8].  
  - "Emissions from third-party AI services, foundation models and AI-enabled software are likely to sit within Scope 3 Category 1" [9].

- **Scope logic under the GHG Protocol**  
  - The **electricity used to run your queries** is **not your Scope 2**; it's the provider's Scope 1/2 and *your* Scope 3 [6].  
  - Services procured from third parties are, by definition, Scope 3 under the Corporate Value Chain Standard.

- **Self-hosted exception**  
  If you **own and operate the hardware** and host AI models internally:
  - The **electricity** used is **Scope 2**.  
  - The **servers' embodied emissions** (manufacture of GPUs/servers) fall in **Scope 3 Category 2 (Capital Goods)** [7].  
  That is still the minority pattern; most organizations buy access to someone else's models.

**Finance-adjacent implication:**  
Treat AI like any other cloud/SaaS: it is **supplier emissions embedded in an opex line item**. In GHG terms, those are Category 1; in finance terms, this is Scope 3 attached to contracts your procurement and FP&A teams already track.

## 3. How AI Usage Shows Up in Different Frameworks (CSRD, SBTi, GHG Updates)

This Category 1 placement isn't just theoretical; it's being pulled into regulatory and target-setting frameworks:

- **CSRD / ESRS E1 (EU)**  
  - ESRS E1 requires Scope 3 disclosure **by category** (including Category 1) and disaggregation of data types (primary, spend-based, etc.).  
  - CSRD guidance and practitioner blogs on AI emissions treat **AI usage as a Scope 3 Category 1 obligation** for companies buying AI as a service.

- **SBTi criteria (2026)**  
  - Near-term SBTi targets must cover at least **67% of Scope 3 emissions**; long-term net-zero targets rise to **90%+** [3][4].  
  - For many professional services and software-heavy businesses, simply **covering Category 1 plus a small handful of other categories** is enough to hit the percentage threshold [3].

- **GHG Protocol Scope 3 revisions (Phase 1)**  
  2026 draft revisions highlight three shifts relevant to AI [10]:
  - **95% inclusion threshold** – organizations must cover 95% of required Scope 3 sources before excluding any, and justify exclusions.  
  - **Mandatory data-type disaggregation** – you must disclose what share of Category 1 is spend-based vs activity-based vs supplier-specific.  
  - **Annual data-quality improvement targets** – Scope 3 becomes a multi-year programme, not a one-off estimate.

**Reading between the lines:**  
If AI shows up as a meaningful spend line (e.g., several million dollars per year or >1–2% of IT opex), it is **very hard to argue AI Category 1 emissions are out of scope** under CSRD/SBTi/GHG revisions.

## 4. Classifying Spend Across Providers  
*(OpenAI vs Azure OpenAI vs Microsoft Copilot vs Anthropic)*

From a Scope 3 perspective, the provider label doesn't change the category—**all of these are Category 1 purchased services**. What *does* change is:

- How usage is billed (per-token, per-seat, embedded in SaaS),
- What **data you can get out** for activity-based calculations,
- How you map each line item into your inventory.

### 4.1 High-level classification

| Spend Type | Category under GHG Protocol | Typical AI Examples | Notes |
|-----------|-----------------------------|---------------------|-------|
| **Direct AI APIs** | Scope 3, Category 1 (Purchased Goods & Services) | OpenAI API, Anthropic Claude API, Azure OpenAI, Google Vertex AI | You buy compute as a service. Provider owns infra, you inherit upstream emissions. |
| **AI-enabled SaaS seats** | Scope 3, Category 1 | Microsoft 365 Copilot, Copilot for Sales, AI features in CRM/ERP | Emissions bundled with seat licenses; no separate API line item on invoice. |
| **Self-hosted models on your own hardware** | Scope 2 (electricity), Scope 3, Category 2 (Capital Goods) | On-prem LLMs, private clusters on owned servers | Only out-of-scope for Category 1 when you truly own and operate the stack. |

Verdantix and other sources note tokens are the main usable activity data [6][9]. | No per-customer CO₂e export. All of these are **upstream purchased services**. The main determinant for your inventory method is not **"OpenAI vs Anthropic"**, but **"API-metered vs seat-bundled"**, because that dictates how granular your activity data can be.

## 5. What Data You Need to Collect (By Provider Type)

From a finance + ESG standpoint, you should design a **data collection template** that your controllers, IT and sustainability team can all use. Recent guidance from AI and CSRD-focused practitioners converges on four core data types [5][6][8][9]:

### 5.1 For direct API usage (OpenAI, Azure OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemini, etc.)

**Minimum, for a credible Category 1 estimate:**

1. **Token usage by model and month**  
   - Input + output tokens (or at least total tokens) per model (e.g., GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet).  
   - Pull this from provider dashboards or billing exports.
2. **Region of compute**  
   - Cloud region (e.g., `eastus`, `westeurope`) where workloads run.  
   - Use this to select appropriate **grid intensity** (g CO₂e/kWh).
3. **Spend by model / project**  
   - Helps with **spend-based cross-check** and ties emissions back to GL accounts.
4. **Task type** (optional but recommended)  
   - Text vs image vs code, high-context vs short prompts; different tasks can have **different energy intensities** [6][13].

**Better, for an activity-based method:**

5. **Energy-per-token estimates per model**  
   - Use published research or vendor-agnostic estimates (e.g., ~0.002–0.01 kWh/query, or per-token values) [6][8][13].  
   - Providers are inconsistent here; you must document your assumptions.
6. **PUE and grid mix data for provider regions**  
   - Microsoft, Google, and Amazon all publish **PUE and energy mix** for their data centers [6][9][11].  
   - Use these to improve the **kWh → CO₂e** conversion.

### 5.2 For seat-licensed AI (Microsoft Copilot, AI-enabled SaaS)

**Minimum, for spend-based Category 1 approximation:**

1. **Seat counts by product and department**  
   - E.g., Copilot seats in Finance vs Legal vs Engineering.  
   - This ties directly into **cost centers**, which finance already tracks.
2. **Annual spend on AI-enabled licenses**  
   - Per product (e.g., Copilot for M365, AI CRM features).  
   - Use **cloud/SaaS emission factors per $ of spend** for a first-pass estimate.

**Better, for more activity-based approaches:**

3. **Usage analytics**  
   - Number of AI prompts or copilot interactions per user or per team (if provided by the SaaS vendor).  
   - Even relative usage (heavy vs light users) can help allocate emissions more fairly.
4. **Vendor sustainability data**  
   - Use Microsoft's ESR and any **Emissions Impact Dashboard** features to anchor your estimates in actual grid mix and efficiency [11][12].

### 5.3 Shadow AI and personal subscriptions

Several 2026 sources stress the **"shadow AI" problem**: employees using personal ChatGPT/Claude accounts, browser-based tools, or unapproved SaaS [6][8]. To capture this:

- **Survey teams** about AI tools used for work.  
- **Consolidate** access to enterprise accounts where possible to pull usage from a single set of dashboards.  
- Apply **conservative spend-based factors** for residual shadow usage you can't fully meter, and document your treatment.

## 6. Recent Metrics and Benchmarks You Can Use

You asked specifically for current numbers that make this concrete. Based on 2025–2026 literature:

- **Per-prompt emissions (order-of-magnitude)**  
  - One 2026 estimate suggests a "typical" ChatGPT prompt is about **0.34 Wh and 0.16 g CO₂e**; a comparable Claude prompt is around **0.35 Wh and 0.21 g CO₂e** [13].  
  - Differences between providers are real but **small compared to usage volume**; how many prompts you run matters more than whether you choose GPT vs Claude.

- **Per-query energy range**  
  - Practitioners suggest **0.002–0.01 kWh per text query** depending on model size and provider efficiency [6][8][9].  
  - This translates roughly to **0.7–3.5 g CO₂e per query** on a 350 g CO₂e/kWh grid, before considering clean power procurement.

- **Enterprise AI spend scale**  
  - Gartner and others estimate global enterprise AI spending will reach **$407B in 2026**, up ~35% from 2025 [15].  
  - U.S. firms are reported to spend ~**$2,068 per employee on AI** in 2026 on average, with professional services sectors reaching >$3,400 per employee [16].

- **Scope 3 dominance**  
  - Scope 3 emissions are typically **80–95% of a company's footprint**, with **Category 1 often the single largest slice** [3][4][17]. AI is small within Category 1 today but growing rapidly as AI budgets balloon [15][16].

These figures are blunt instruments, but they give your CFO and CSO something to sanity-check against their internal estimates.

## 8. Bridge to the "How to Calculate" Piece

This exploration has answered **"where does AI fit?"**. The natural next question is **"how do we actually calculate it?"**

Your follow-on "How to Calculate AI Emissions" article can pick up on three threads from here:

1. **Choice of method** (per GHG Protocol):  
   - **Spend-based**:  
     - Equation: *AI emissions = AI spend × SaaS/cloud emission factor per $*  
     - Fast, low-accuracy; good for first-year screening.  
   - **Activity-based**:  
     - Equation: *Emissions = tokens × kWh/token × grid intensity*  
     - Uses token logs, model energy profiles, and regional grid data [5][6][8].  
   - **Supplier-specific / hybrid**:  
     - Over time, combine **provider disclosures**, your own logs, and carbon accounting platforms that track **per-token emissions ledger entries** [9].

2. **Data quality hierarchy under 2026 GHG revisions**:  
   - Map which AI contracts can realistically move from **spend-based → activity-based → supplier-specific** over the next 2–3 reporting cycles [10].

3. **Worked examples**:  
   - "One team, one model, one year": from tokens to tonnes.  
   - "Copilot for 1,000 users": estimating emissions when all you see is a SaaS license line.

You can end this piece by explicitly pointing forward:

> "In the next article, we'll take these same providers—OpenAI, Azure OpenAI, Microsoft Copilot, Anthropic—and walk through actual formulas and example calculations. The goal is not perfection on day one, but a **documented, finance-grade methodology** you can defend under CSRD, SBTi and the 2026 GHG Protocol revisions."
