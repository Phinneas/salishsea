**Only 20% of consumers believe brands accurately describe their sustainability efforts.** That's from a February 2025 survey of more than 5,000 shoppers in the US, UK, France, Germany, Australia and New Zealand. Another 26% flatly distrust them.

But impact claims can sell. Across 600,000 US products, the ones carrying ESG claims grew 28% over five years. Products without them grew 20%.

So the claim isn't the problem. The story around it usually is. Here are the five places impact stories break, and a self-check for each.

> **What this page diagnoses:** why a mission-driven brand's impact story builds goodwill but not sales.
> **Who it's for:** marketing leads and founders at sustainable consumer brands.
> **Key sources:** Blue Yonder consumer survey (Feb 2025, n = 5,000+, five markets), McKinsey and NielsenIQ (Feb 2023, ~600,000 SKUs, ~$400B in US retail revenue, 2017 to mid-2022), PwC Voice of the Consumer Survey (2024).
> **Last checked:** October 15, 2026.

---

## The Short Answer

Most impact stories are built to make people admire the brand. Conversion needs something else: proof at the moment of doubt, at a price the buyer has already budgeted for.

The five breaks, strongest first:

1. **The proof isn't where the doubt is.**
2. **The premium is bigger than the buyer's green budget.**
3. **The story is about you, not the buyer's choice.**
4. **You have one claim doing all the work.**
5. **The story only talks to the slow, deliberate brain.**

---

## 1. The Proof Isn't Where the Doubt Is

Doubt shows up on the product page and in the cart. Most impact stories live on the About page.

In the Blue Yonder survey, 30% of shoppers said there wasn't enough sustainability information about the product itself. Another 26% pointed to unclear or misleading claims. They aren't asking for a better mission statement. They're asking for a fact they can check, next to the thing they're about to buy.

**Self-check:** Open your best-selling product page. Is there one specific, checkable impact fact within a scroll of the Add to Cart button? A number, a method, or a named certifier counts. "Sustainably made" doesn't.

---

## 2. The Premium Is Bigger Than the Buyer's Green Budget

Shoppers don't pay for sustainability out of their whole budget. They pay out of a smaller mental account for it. PwC put the average willingness to pay at about 9.7% over the conventional price. In the Blue Yonder survey, the most common answer was 5% to 9.9%. And 54% named higher cost as a barrier.

If your price sits 25% above the conventional option, the story has to carry the gap. It usually can't.

**Self-check:** Compare your price to the closest conventional alternative. If the gap is over 10%, does the page explain what the extra money buys in plain terms? Durability, cost per use and refill savings all count. Impact alone rarely does.

---

## 3. The Story Is About You, Not the Buyer's Choice

"Founded to fight plastic waste" is a story about the brand. "This bottle replaces 300 single-use ones" is a story about the purchase. Only the second one helps a buyer decide.

Brand stories earn trust. Purchase stories earn the click. Most sustainable brands have plenty of the first and almost none of the second.

**Self-check:** Read your homepage hero and your top product description. Count the sentences where the subject is "we." Count the ones where the subject is the product or the buyer. If "we" wins, rewrite one sentence per page.

---

## 4. You Have One Claim Doing All the Work

In the McKinsey and NielsenIQ data, products with more than one type of ESG claim grew about twice as fast as products with only one. Less common claims also tended to show bigger growth gaps than the most common ones.

A single "eco-friendly" badge reads like a checkbox. Two or three distinct, specific claims start to read like a pattern.

**Self-check:** List every distinct impact claim on your top product. Are there at least two different types? Materials, packaging, sourcing and end of life are different types. Three ways of saying "sustainable" are one.

---

## 5. The Story Only Talks to the Slow Brain

Impact stories are written for the careful reader who compares supply chains. Most purchases happen fast, on habit and familiarity. A story that needs three paragraphs to land loses to a competitor the buyer already knows.

**Self-check:** Can a shopper get your strongest impact point in under five seconds, from an image, a label or a single line? If it only works when someone reads closely, it won't work at checkout.

---

## Score Yourself

| Break | Pass if... | Pass / Fail |
|---|---|---|
| Proof at the doubt point | A checkable fact sits near Add to Cart | |
| Premium vs. green budget | Gap is under 10%, or the page explains the extra value | |
| Buyer-centered story | Product and buyer sentences outnumber "we" sentences | |
| Multiple claim types | Two or more distinct claim types on the top product | |
| Fast-brain version | Strongest point lands in five seconds | |

**4 or 5 passes:** Your story isn't the bottleneck. Look at traffic quality or price.
**2 or 3 passes:** Fix the proof and the premium first. They have the clearest evidence behind them.
**0 or 1 pass:** You have a brand story and no purchase story. Start with break 3.
