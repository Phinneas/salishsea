Most small sustainability-focused brands are bracing for a compliance deadline that was never going to reach them, while the legal exposure that already applies to them today gets almost no attention.

The SEC's climate disclosure rule is the one that generates the anxiety — it's the headline regulation, it's what shows up in "ESG reporting requirements" search results, and it's easy to assume that "climate disclosure is now federal law" eventually trickles down to every company with a sustainability claim on its packaging. It doesn't, and as of this writing it may not apply to anyone at all. Meanwhile, the one federal rule that has no revenue floor and no exemption for small or private companies — the FTC's authority over deceptive environmental marketing claims — rarely appears in the same conversation, even though it's the rule actually being enforced against companies your size, right now.

Sorting that out — what's genuinely required, what's a voluntary certification with real costs and real signaling value, and what independent research says actually moves a skeptical customer or investor — is the difference between spending a year and several thousand dollars solving the wrong problem, and spending that time on the two or three things that would actually move trust.

## Required, Federal: The SEC Rule That Was Never Aimed at You

The SEC adopted its climate-related disclosure rule on March 6, 2024, and the rule was ambitious — mandatory Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions disclosure, climate risk governance, and financial statement impacts from severe weather, for public companies. But even in its final, most demanding form, the rule explicitly exempted smaller reporting companies and emerging growth companies from any greenhouse gas emissions disclosure obligation whatsoever. Only large accelerated filers and accelerated filers — the SEC's own estimate put this at roughly 40% of domestic filers and 60% of foreign private issuers, about 7,000 and 900 companies respectively — were ever on the hook, and Scope 3 (the category that would have captured a small brand's own emissions as *someone else's* supply-chain disclosure) was dropped from the rule entirely before it was even finalized.

The rule never got the chance to apply to anyone. On April 4, 2024, the SEC stayed it pending consolidated litigation in the Eighth Circuit. On March 27, 2025, the SEC itself voted to stop defending the rule in court. On May 29, 2026, the agency went further and formally proposed rescinding it outright, arguing in its Federal Register filing that the rule "exceed[ed] the scope of the agency's statutory authority." The public comment period on that rescission closed August 3, 2026. As of this writing, no final action has been taken — the rule is neither in force nor formally repealed, suspended in the same procedural limbo it's occupied for most of its existence.

The practical upshot for a small brand: this was never your compliance deadline, and the regulation with your name plausibly attached to it is now more likely to be erased than enforced. If your team has a "prepare for SEC climate disclosure" line item on a roadmap, it's solving a problem that, structurally, was never assigned to companies your size.

## Required, State: Real, but Still Not Aimed at You Either

California's two climate disclosure statutes are worth knowing about precisely because they're more durable than the SEC rule, not because they reach small companies. SB 253 (the Climate Corporate Data Accountability Act) applies to entities with at least $1 billion in annual revenue doing business in California — Scope 1 and 2 disclosure is on track for August 10, 2026, with Scope 3 following in 2027 or later. SB 261 (the Climate-Related Financial Risk Act) sets a $500 million threshold and, as of a March 2026 update, is on hold under a Ninth Circuit injunction with no ruling timeline announced. "Doing business in California" is defined broadly enough to catch out-of-state companies with meaningful California sales, so it's worth watching if a brand is scaling toward nine figures in revenue — but at the size range these laws describe, a company has an in-house or outside counsel team already tracking this. It is not, realistically, the compliance risk facing a founder-run sustainable CPG or apparel brand.

## The Requirement With No Size Floor: What You Say, Not What You File

Here is the rule that actually applies regardless of revenue: Section 5 of the FTC Act, enforced alongside the FTC's Green Guides and equivalent state unfair-and-deceptive-practices statutes, which prohibit a marketing claim that would mislead a reasonable consumer — with no company-size exemption anywhere in the statute. The enforcement record bears this out. Reynolds Consumer Products settled with Arizona's attorney general for $212,000 in February 2026 over "recyclable" claims on Hefty-branded bags. Philadelphia sued SC Johnson and Bimbo Bakeries in September 2025 over the same category of claim on flexible-film packaging. California's attorney general settled with four separate plastic-bag manufacturers for a combined $1.75 million in October 2025 — companies that, unlike Coca-Cola or Nike, are not household names, under a state truth-in-recyclability statute that doesn't require proving intent, only that the claim was misleading in the market where it shipped.

None of these companies filed anything with the SEC over the claims that got them sued. The exposure that reaches a small brand isn't disclosure-shaped; it's claim-shaped. It shows up on a product label, an Instagram caption, a homepage headline — anywhere the words "sustainable," "recyclable," or "eco-friendly" get printed without the specific fact behind them being checked against reality first, which is [the same failure pattern documented across every one of these settlements](/blog/what-greenwashing-actually-is/).

## Optional: B Corp, After the 2026 Standards Overhaul

B Corp certification changed structurally in 2026, and most existing guidance to small brands still describes the version that no longer applies. The old B Impact Assessment let a company reach a cumulative score of 80 out of 200 points, meaning genuine weakness in one area — say, labor practices — could be offset by strength in another, like environmental packaging. The new "V2" standard eliminates that compensation entirely: every certifying company must now clear a mandatory minimum bar, independently, across seven fixed Impact Topics — Purpose & Stakeholder Governance, Climate Action, Human Rights, Fair Work, Justice/Equity/Diversity & Inclusion, Environmental Stewardship & Circularity, and Government Affairs & Collective Action. Climate Action specifically requires a 1.5°C-aligned climate plan, and that requirement applies at every company size, not just to large filers the way SEC and California thresholds do.

The certification cycle also changed shape, from a flat three-year renewal to a five-year cycle with escalating checkpoints: baseline "Year 0" requirements to certify, additional sub-requirements activating at Year 3, and final obligations at Year 5 — converting certification from a one-time achievement into an ongoing, auditable improvement obligation. B Lab's 2026 fee schedule scales with revenue: $2,100 a year for companies under $5 million in revenue, rising to $3,150 at $5–7.5 million, $6,300 at $10–15 million, and up through $16,800 a year at $30–50 million — plus, new for 2026, pass-through fees funding independent third-party audits that didn't exist under the prior system. Realistic time-to-certify runs at least 12 months, and the documentation burden — collecting evidence across all seven topics with no ability to lean on a strength to cover a gap — lands disproportionately hard on small teams without dedicated sustainability staff. B Corp is a real, meaningfully strengthened standard. It is also now a heavier lift for a five-person company than the "quiz-and-fee" reputation it earned a decade ago.

## Optional: 1% for the Planet — a Narrower, Lighter Commitment

1% for the Planet asks for something much more specific and bounded: at least 1% of a member's actual earned annual revenue (gross revenue minus returns, discounts, and pass-through costs), given to approved environmental nonprofit partners, with at least half of that commitment paid in cash — the remainder can be in-kind product or service donations, volunteer hours (valued at $29.95/hour), or approved advertising featuring the 1% for the Planet mark. Compliance isn't self-attested: members must certify within 120 days of their fiscal year-end, submitting proof of revenue (tax documents, accountant letters, or bookkeeping reports) alongside receipts or acknowledgment letters documenting the actual donations, reviewed by the organization's certification team before continued membership is confirmed.

This is a materially lighter commitment than B Corp — it governs giving, not the whole operating structure of the company — which makes it a more realistic starting point for a very small team, though it's also a narrower signal: it says a brand donates on schedule, not that its supply chain, labor practices, and governance have cleared an independent bar.

| | B Corp (V2, 2026) | 1% for the Planet |
|---|---|---|
| **What's assessed** | Operations, governance, labor, climate plan, supply chain — 7 mandatory topics | Annual giving only (≥1% of revenue) |
| **Verification** | Independent third-party audit, phased over 5 years | Annual document review (revenue + donation proof) |
| **Annual cost (small brand)** | $2,100+/year (under $5M revenue) plus audit pass-through fees | 1% of revenue, ≥50% as cash |
| **Time to first certify** | ~12 months | Single certification cycle, annual renewal |
| **Best fit** | Brands ready to rebuild processes around a documented standard | Brands wanting a bounded, verifiable giving commitment now |

## What Actually Moves Trust — the Evidence Beyond the Badge

The honest answer to "does certification work" is more specific than yes or no, and it comes with a timing detail most founders don't expect.

A 2025 *Journal of Business Ethics* study examined 748 sustainability-focused ventures, matching 374 B Corp–certified companies against 374 comparable non-certified ones using propensity score matching to isolate the certification's effect from other differences between the groups. Certification measurably increased funding — but the effect was strongest for early-stage ventures and weakened as companies matured. The mechanism the authors describe is classic signaling theory: certification works because it's costly (a company has to commit real time, money, and structural change to earn it), credible (an independent third party, not the company itself, is vouching for it), and observable (an investor can verify it exists without doing their own diligence). For a small brand, this means the funding-relevant value of certification is front-loaded — it does the most work exactly when a company has the least of its own track record to point to instead, and progressively less as the company builds a longer independent history investors can evaluate directly.

That's a reason to certify early if a brand is raising capital, not a reason to assume the badge alone carries the argument indefinitely. Separate research on claim specificity — a 2026 study in the *Journal of Sustainable Marketing* testing otherwise-identical sustainability labels — found that adding a checkable methodology description cut perceived greenwashing by roughly 40% and measurably improved brand evaluation, without changing the underlying claim at all. The lesson generalizes past ad copy to certification badges themselves: a B Corp or 1% for the Planet logo with no specific, current number behind it (this year's actual score, this year's actual donation total) reads to a skeptical customer the same way an unqualified "sustainable" label does — a claim without the fact attached. The badge opens the door. The specific number is what keeps a skeptical reader from discounting the badge itself.

## A Practical Framework, in Order

**Check whether you're actually required to do anything.** For the overwhelming majority of small, privately held sustainable brands, the answer is no at the federal level (SEC rule pending rescission, never applied to your size class regardless) and no at the state level unless you're approaching nine figures in revenue with meaningful California sales.

**Audit your marketing claims against the standard that does apply.** Every "recyclable," "sustainable," or "eco-friendly" claim on packaging, a homepage, or a product page should survive the same reasonable-consumer test that cost Reynolds $212,000 and four California bag manufacturers $1.75 million combined — regardless of your revenue.

**Decide on voluntary certification based on your stage, not your values.** If you're actively raising capital, the funding-signal effect of B Corp certification is real and front-loaded to exactly this stage, per the 748-venture study above — worth the ~12-month process and the $2,100-plus annual cost if the timing lines up with a raise. If you're not raising and want a bounded, verifiable commitment now, 1% for the Planet is the lighter lift.

**Whatever you choose, publish the specific number next to the badge.** A certification logo with no current data point attached is the same category of claim the specificity research says gets discounted. The badge plus this year's actual score, actual donation total, or actual emissions number is what a skeptical reader — customer or investor — can't wave away.
