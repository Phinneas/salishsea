# Design What If

A four-step pipeline for turning a directory or content site into a single, emotionally
specific design brief. Based on the principle that great design commits fully to one idea
and resolves a real emotional tension, rather than listing features or information.

## The Pipeline

```
STEP 1: Hidden Truth   → three diagnostic questions
STEP 2: What If         → reframe built from the hidden truth
STEP 3: The Feel        → emotion that resolves the named tension
STEP 4: Combine         → one sentence that governs every design decision
```

---

## Step 1 — Find the Hidden Truth

Don't start with what the product does on the surface. Ask three questions, in order:

1. **What is the real problem?** (not the surface category — what's actually going on for the user)
2. **What is the consequence?** (what happens, or what's at stake, because of that problem)
3. **What is the emotional impact?** (how the consequence actually feels)

The answer to question 3 is usually both the Hidden Truth and a strong candidate for Step 3
(The Feel) — they're often the same insight asked twice, once diagnostically and once as a
design target.

**Worked example — BinLocator.co:**
1. Real problem → liquidation shoppers don't know if a trip is worth it before they go
2. Consequence → they either skip a good haul or waste a trip on nothing
3. Emotional impact → anxiety about wasted effort, masked as excitement about the hunt

Hidden Truth: the mystery creates real risk, and the site's job is to make that risk feel
worth taking — not just to lean into chaos for its own sake.

**Audience note:** Strong hidden truths often borrow meaning that already exists in the
audience's own head — language or feelings they already have, not concepts invented for them.
Speak to what they already think, don't introduce a new idea.

---

## Step 2 — Write the What If

A strong "What If" reframes the entire site as if its hero feature (the single most defining,
non-obvious trait) WAS the product — not just a feature it has.

> **What if [site] was [reframe built from the hidden truth], rather than [surface category]?**

**The three-decision test:** answering yes must force at least three simultaneous design
decisions — visual language, copy voice, and information hierarchy — to change. If it only
changes one thing, it's a feature idea, not a reframe.

| Weak (feature idea) | Strong (reframe) |
|---|---|
| What if we showed elevation on listing cards? | What if the listing card was structured like a trailhead sign rather than a hotel review? |
| What if we used a warmer color palette? | What if HarvestMap was a seasonal family ritual planner rather than a farm finder? |

**Alternate route into Step 2 — Literal Interpretation:**
If the hidden-truth route doesn't produce a strong reframe, try this instead: find a metaphor,
idiom, or common phrase your audience already uses about this category, and represent it
*literally* rather than abstractly. Don't invent new meaning — borrow meaning the audience
already carries and make it visible.

Reference example (Bloomberg Businessweek): instead of charts or data to represent a "bull
market," the cover rendered an actual, physical bull. Investors already own that metaphor
subconsciously — making it literal creates instant recognition with zero explanation needed.

Applied to Buzz's portfolio: u-pick visitors already say "let's make a day of it" — HarvestMap
could render that literally as a day-planner structure rather than a map. Cold plunge/sauna
users already talk about "shocking the system" — ContrastMap could lean into that literal
shock-and-reset visual rhythm rather than a wellness aesthetic.

---

## Step 3 — Name the Feel

Pick a single emotion the visitor should carry away. Organize by register — the category
tells you what design language family you're working in.

**Longing** — Wistful, Nostalgic, Anticipatory, Bittersweet
**Calm** — Restored, Unhurried, Settled, Safe
**Alive** — Energized, Curious, Discovered, Awake
**Belonging** — Seen, Known, Understood, Like an insider
**Permission** — Relieved, Unburdened, Justified, Free
**Delight** — Surprised, Charmed, Amused, Warm

**Selection rule:** don't just pick what sounds positive. Pick the emotion that directly
**resolves the tension named in Step 1's emotional impact answer.** The strongest Feel choice
answers the specific burden identified in the Hidden Truth, not generic positivity.

- Sounddip resolves depletion → **Unburdened**
- HarvestMap resolves disconnection → **Warm**
- KeystonePlants resolves guilt → **Justified**
- BinLocator resolves wasted-effort anxiety → likely **Rewarded** or **Vindicated**, not just "Excited"

---

## Step 4 — Combine

Merge the What If and the Feel into one governing sentence:

> **"[What If reframe] — so that visitors leave feeling [chosen emotion]."**

This sentence becomes the single design brief. Test every design decision against it:
does this choice serve the sentence, or add weight against it?

---

## Output Format

```
PROJECT: [Site name]

STEP 1 — HIDDEN TRUTH
Real problem: [...]
Consequence: [...]
Emotional impact: [...]

STEP 2 — WHAT IF
What if [site] was [reframe] rather than [surface category]?

STEP 3 — THE FEEL
[Emotion] — resolves [the named tension from Step 1]

STEP 4 — COMBINED BRIEF
"[What If] — so that visitors leave feeling [emotion]."

DOWNSTREAM DECISIONS
1. Visual language → [specific change]
2. Copy voice → [specific change]
3. Information hierarchy → [specific change]
[4+. Optional: navigation logic, empty states, listing card structure]
```

## Examples from Buzz's Portfolio

**Sounddip (sound bath directory)**
- Hidden truth: visitors are depleted — overstimulated, anxious, or recovering. Not shopping
  for wellness, looking for permission to stop.
- What If: What if Sounddip was a depletion recovery tool rather than a sound bath directory?
- Feel: **Unburdened**
- Combined: "What if Sounddip was a depletion recovery tool rather than a sound bath directory
  — so that visitors leave feeling unburdened."
- Downstream:
  1. Visual language → maximum whitespace, slow transitions, the quietest possible UI
  2. Copy voice → "An hour where nothing is required of you" / listings describe what you
     don't have to do, not what's on offer
  3. Information hierarchy → session length and sensory environment lead / instructor
     credentials and price follow

**HarvestMap.co (u-pick farm directory)**
- Hidden truth: u-pick is a family ritual, not a shopping trip. People go for the experience
  of picking together, not the efficiency of acquiring produce.
- What If: What if HarvestMap was a seasonal family ritual planner rather than a farm finder?
- Feel: **Warm**
- Combined: "What if HarvestMap was a seasonal family ritual planner rather than a farm finder
  — so that visitors leave feeling warm."
- Downstream:
  1. Visual language → warm, analog aesthetic — hand-drawn elements, imperfect textures
  2. Copy voice → "Make it a Saturday" replaces "Find a farm" / listings describe the
     experience, not the acreage
  3. Information hierarchy → "best for kids," "bring a cooler," and "what's ripe now" move
     above GPS coordinates and hours

**KeystonePlants.co (native plant nursery directory)**
- Hidden truth: buyers feel mild guilt about their non-native yard and want incremental
  recovery, not overwhelming commitment.
- What If: What if KeystonePlants was a yard recovery guide that connected you with nurseries,
  rather than a nursery directory that mentions native plants?
- Feel: **Justified**
- Combined: "What if KeystonePlants was a yard recovery guide rather than a nursery directory
  — so that visitors leave feeling justified."
- Downstream:
  1. Visual language → before/after framing in cards — what a yard supports now vs. what it
     could support with one change
  2. Copy voice → "Replace one lawn section this fall" replaces "Browse nurseries" /
     guilt-free, incremental language throughout
  3. Information hierarchy → ecological impact score leads every listing / nursery details follow

## Notes for Buzz

- Run this full pipeline *before* briefing Claude Code or Aider — Step 4's combined sentence
  becomes the one-line constraint that governs the build
- A reframe that only changes one design decision is a feature, not a concept
- The Feel selection rule: pick the emotion that resolves the tension, not the one that
  sounds nicest
- Niche language (speaking the audience's own words back to them) strengthens both the What If
  and the Feel — borrow meaning that already exists in their heads rather than inventing new language
- Minimalism check: once you have the combined sentence, every element on the page should earn
  its place against it — if it doesn't serve the sentence, cut it
